A hive is a small group of friends who walk and check in with each other. **Three to eight** people is the sweet spot: big enough that someone is always walking, small enough that a quiet day is noticeable. That is the idea. A public leaderboard of strangers is a different sport. The same shape works in BuzzBuzz, and it works in any group chat you already have.

## Why a small group beats a giant board

In a group of five, a missed day has a name. A friend did not send the photo. You know that friend, and the chat is usually noisy by dinner, so the gap shows up without a chart.

On a public leaderboard the same miss is a blank row. A stranger who walked more fills the slot. Nobody is waiting on a message from you, so the silence is free. Tomorrow’s rank goes to whoever posts. They will not notice you were gone.

Three to eight keeps the notice working. The group is big enough that a normal Tuesday still has a walker in it, and small enough that one missing check-in does not vanish in the scroll. Two people can hold a hive until one of them travels, and then you are talking to yourself. Past eight, a missing friend blends into the thread the way a missing name blends into a long board. The totals keep moving. The “where were you?” stops.

The big thread you muted does not ask where the quiet person went. The four-person thread still does. Keep the group small enough that someone still asks.

## What a hive day looks like

You walk, or you get enough steps to clear a floor the group agreed on. Then one check-in: a photo, a step count, or a single “done.” Steps are the score. The picture is the story.

For a mixed group, 5,000–8,000 steps is a strong first week. A shift, a kid, or a long commute can still clear it. A shorter day counts as showing up. A [30-minute walk is about 3,000 steps](/guides/how-many-steps-is-a-30-minute-walk) at a steady pace. Treat the 3,000 as an estimate. Hills, strollers, and red lights move it, and the check-in stays one message.

The photo can be dull. A bakery window, a weird cloud, a dog in a coat, the corner you already pass on the way home. People open the picture. The step count can sit underneath. In BuzzBuzz that photo stays up for about a day, then leaves, so yesterday does not pile up. A group chat can do the same job. Send one picture and move on.

Morning and evening both count. One friend can loop the block at 7 a.m. Another can walk after the school run. The hive shares the check-in. Start times can differ, and the day counts when the check-in shows up.

## What a hive is not

A hive stays small. It is a few friends, a daily step count, and one check-in light enough to repeat. [Why we made a step counter with bees](/guides/why-we-made-a-step-counter-with-bees) names the pieces left out on purpose: no arena of strangers, and no feed that keeps every photo forever.

A coach and a calorie counter stay out too. The group is there to notice the walk. Nobody is owed a meal plan, a speech, or a new personality. The walk is allowed to be ordinary.

## How to start one this week

1. **Invite people who already like you.** Three to eight friends who answer your texts.
2. **Pick a floor you can hit.** 5,000–8,000 steps for week one. Raise it later, only after the check-ins are still arriving.
3. **Agree on one check-in.** A photo, a step count, or a single “done.” One proof a day.
4. **Name the group.** A silly name lasts longer than “Fitness Challenge 2.”
5. **Start tomorrow morning.** Starting “sometime this week” is how a hive stays a planning message.

Send one plain text and pin it. “6,000 steps. One photo. We start tomorrow. No speeches.” If the rule needs a paragraph, it is already too heavy.
