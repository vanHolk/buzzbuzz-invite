Yes. If your phone or watch is on you, the steps from a hike count the same way sidewalk steps do. The counter is tallying footfalls. A climb and a heavy pack change how hard those steps feel. The number on the screen stays a count of steps.

People ask it every fall, and again in the spring. The count works the same in April as it does in October.

## What gets counted

A step counter counts footfalls, on a trail or on a sidewalk. You moved, and it added.

Apple Watch lists [Hiking as a workout type](https://support.apple.com/en-us/105089). During a workout, [raising your wrist can show elapsed time, average pace, distance covered, calories burned, and heart rate](https://support.apple.com/guide/watch/start-a-workout-apdd16e8761a/watchos). That is the workout screen. The day's step total sits in the Activity app. Scroll past the rings and you get [total steps, total distance, and flights climbed](https://support.apple.com/guide/watch/track-daily-activity-apd3bf6d85a6/watchos).

The phone has to come with you. Leave it in the car and the trail never reaches the sensor. A phone in the lid of a pack can finish lower than a watch on a wrist. A phone in a bag and a watch on a wrist are both guessing from movement. Carry the one you want the day's count to come from.

## Why a hike feels harder

Take 6,000 steps up a hill and 6,000 steps on flat pavement. Both afternoons show 6,000. The hill is the one that runs out of conversation first.

The [CDC's current intensity page](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/measuring/index.html) uses a talk test for that difference. Moderate effort means you can talk but not sing. Vigorous effort means you can say only a few words before you need a breath. Brisk walking, 2.5 miles an hour or faster, is the moderate example there.

[Hiking uphill or with a heavy backpack](https://www.cdc.gov/physicalactivity/basics/measuring/index.html) is the vigorous example on the CDC's earlier intensity page. [Table 4-1 of the Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans](https://health.gov/sites/default/files/2019-09/Physical_Activity_Guidelines_2nd_edition.pdf) lists that same hike as vigorous, in the table that also lists brisk walking at 2.5 miles an hour or faster. Vigorous describes the effort. The screen still shows the steps you took.

## Steps on a trail

[One mile is often about 2,000–2,500 steps](/guides/how-many-steps-is-1-mile). That range comes from step length on ordinary ground. On a steep or rocky trail you may notice shorter steps, so the same distance can take more of them. Check it the next time you already know the distance. One trail mile from your own shoes is the number to keep.

Time is the other half. [Walking 10,000 steps usually takes about 90–120 minutes](/guides/how-long-to-walk-10000-steps) of actual walking. Climbs and viewpoint stops spend minutes that never become steps. The pace chart already lives in that guide. Use it, then trust the counter you wore.

## A hike day with friends

Two to six friends. One trail. Everyone posts a step count and one photo from the highest point they reached.

Most flights climbed wins, when the tracker already shows flights. If it does not, the step count is the score. The slowest walker picks the snack stop. Pin the rule in one sentence. "One trail, one photo from the high point, steps underneath, slowest walker chooses snacks."

A [fall color walk](/guides/fall-color-walk) is the shorter outing, and those photo prompts still work once the path turns to dirt. A red leaf counts on a switchback. If Saturday goes well and you want the habit to outlast one trailhead, [start a walking streak with friends](/guides/start-a-walking-streak-with-friends). Same people. A smaller check-in on ordinary days.

If you use BuzzBuzz, a hive can hold the day's tally: a few friends, a daily step count, and one photo. A group chat you already have will do the same job.
