Your iPhone and Apple Watch each count steps on their own. When both write steps, the source at the top of Data Sources & Access takes priority in [Apple Health](https://support.apple.com/en-us/108779). Health does not add the two devices together. The phone also misses the walking you do while it sits on a desk, and the two devices count a little differently on the same walk. Small gaps are normal. A big sudden drop usually means the wrong source is on top.

## How Apple Health picks a winner

The Health app takes similar data from different sources and organizes it by where it came from. By default, Apple prioritizes in this order:

1. Health data that you enter manually.
2. Data from your iPhone, iPad, and Apple Watch.
3. Data from apps and Bluetooth devices.

If several sources contribute the same data type, the one at the top of the list takes priority over the others. A new data source appears above the apps and devices already contributing. New apps or devices are listed at the top automatically, above your iPhone or iPad, so a fitness app allowed to write steps can become the source Health trusts.

## Put the right source on top

On iPhone:

1. Open the Health app.
2. Tap the Browse tab.
3. Tap a category, then a subcategory. For steps, that is Activity, then Steps.
4. Scroll down, then tap Data Sources & Access. Only the sources that contribute to that data type appear.
5. Tap Edit.
6. Touch and hold the Change Order button next to the device you carry or wear all day, and drag it to the top. If you wear a watch all day, put Apple Watch there.
7. To turn off a third-party source, tap the checkmark next to it.
8. Tap Done.

## Health Connect on Android

[Health Connect](https://support.google.com/android/answer/12990553) merges steps when several apps write the same data type. Where those apps conflict, the one higher on the priority list wins. In [Google’s example](https://support.google.com/android/answer/13770384), two fitness apps both write steps from 8:00 AM to 9:00 AM, and Health Connect takes the count from the higher-priority app.

Open Health Connect. Under Permissions and data, tap Manage Data, then Data sources and priority. Under App sources, touch and hold next to the app name and drag the handle. To leave an app out of the totals, next to Data sources and priority, tap Edit, then Remove. The app can still write. Those steps do not count in the totals.

Some apps read all of the data and merge it their own way, so two apps can show different totals for the same day.

## Keep one step tally with BuzzBuzz

Once you’ve sorted out which device to trust, pick one tally for comparing your walks. BuzzBuzz tracks your daily steps and shows progress toward a step goal. Try checking its tally before and after the same loop a few times. The change gives you a number you can compare from walk to walk, and you can stop refereeing the phone and watch after every outing.

[Download BuzzBuzz on the App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/buzzbuzz-step-tracker/id6767195595) or [get it on Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.buzzbuzz.steptracker).

## Same walk, two devices

A phone counts steps while you carry it. Leave it on a desk, in a bag, or on a charger, and that stretch is missing from the phone. A watch counts from the wrist, so it keeps the walk the phone sat out. The list can be in the right order and the totals can still disagree.

## How big a gap is ordinary

Case, Burwick, Volpp, and Patel compared devices with a person counting steps, in [JAMA (2015)](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2108876). Fourteen adults walked on a treadmill at 3.0 mph, for 500 steps and for 1,500 steps, each twice. The relative difference in the mean step count ranged from **−6.7% to +6.2%** for the smartphone apps, and from **−22.7% to −1.5%** for the wearable devices worn on the wrist. The adults were young and healthy, the devices were a short 2014 list, and the walk was on a treadmill. The authors say to confirm those ranges in other settings.

On an 8,000-step day, as an illustration, 6% is 8,000 × 0.06 = **480 steps**. A few hundred steps of disagreement sits inside the phone range they measured. At the low end of those wrist devices, 22.7% of 8,000 is 1,816, about **1,800 steps**. A drop well past that, or a total that falls the day a new app appears, is a reason to open the source list.

## Count 500 steps and compare

Carry the phone and wear the watch. Walk and count **500 steps** out loud, then set each reading next to your count. A known distance works too. [How many steps are in 1 mile](/guides/how-many-steps-is-1-mile) has the method: note the counter at the start and at the end of a mile you already know, and subtract. A [30-minute walk](/guides/how-many-steps-is-a-30-minute-walk) is a longer test, about 3,000 steps at a steady pace. Keep the closer device, the one you have on you most of the day, and drag it to the top of the list.

## In a hive, compare the week

A [hive](/guides/what-is-a-hive) often mixes someone wearing a watch with a friend who only carries a phone. A 212-step win on Tuesday is a bad prize. Compare the week. Pick one source and leave it, so your own days stay comparable. A [walking streak with friends](/guides/start-a-walking-streak-with-friends) cares whether everyone walked, not whose device counted 3% higher.
