Trick-or-treating is roughly **2,000 steps** for every mile of route for an adult, plus extra steps for every driveway and porch. On the driveway example below, a one-mile loop lands nearer **3,000 steps**. That second figure is an estimate. It moves with your step length and with how far each front door sits from the sidewalk. Kids take more steps than the adult beside them on the same streets, because a shorter step covers less ground.

## Map the streets, then multiply

Draw the loop in a map app before you leave, read the miles, and multiply.

The [University of Nebraska Medical Center’s walking guide](https://www.unmc.edu/cfhl/resources/walking.html) puts an average stride at about **2.5 feet** and says a mile takes **just over 2,000 steps**. A mile is 5,280 feet, and 5,280 ÷ 2.5 = **2,112 steps**. [How many steps are in 1 mile](/guides/how-many-steps-is-1-mile) has the same formula for a shorter or longer step.

On that figure, the street portion scales like this:

- 1 mile is 1 × 2,112 = **2,112 steps**, about **2,100**
- 2 miles is 2 × 2,112 = **4,224 steps**, about **4,200**
- 3 miles is 3 × 2,112 = **6,336 steps**, about **6,300**

Those rows count the sidewalk. Each trip from the curb to a door sits on top of them.

## The driveways sit off the map

Each house is an out-and-back from the sidewalk to the door. Add that distance yourself. The line in the map app follows the street.

This worked example is an estimate you can replace with your own block. A **40-foot** walk from the sidewalk to the door, then the same 40 feet back, is **80 feet**. At 2.5 feet a step, 80 ÷ 2.5 = **32 steps** for that house. Thirty houses at that length are 30 × 32 = **960 steps**. Those steps cover 960 × 2.5 = **2,400 feet**, and 2,400 ÷ 5,280 is about **0.45 miles**, near half a mile on top of the street line.

A short city stoop is a smaller out-and-back. A long suburban driveway is a larger one. Pace the walk to one door and back, divide those feet by 2.5, and multiply by the doors you expect to ring.

One mile of streets is 2,112 steps. Add the 960-step porch example and the night is 2,112 + 960 = **3,072 steps**, nearer **3,000**.

## Count the moving minutes

Waiting at a door, sorting the candy, and re-pinning a cape all leave the step count where it is. Two hours from the first door to the last includes a lot of standing.

In [CADENCE-Adults](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12966-019-0769-6) (Tudor-Locke and colleagues, 2019), healthy adults ages 21 to 40 walked on a treadmill, and the published moderate-intensity threshold was rounded to about **100 steps a minute**. That figure is a lab reference for intensity. [A 30-minute walk](/guides/how-many-steps-is-a-30-minute-walk) uses the same marker. For a time sketch of this night, count only the minutes your feet were moving and multiply by about 100. Forty moving minutes is 40 × 100 = **about 4,000 steps**. That product is an estimate. It assumes the cadence stayed near the marker.

The [NHS describes a brisk walk as about 3 miles an hour](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/walking-for-health/). At that pace, one mile takes about **20 minutes** of walking. The mapped miles and the moving minutes are the two inputs that turn into steps.

## Kids finish with a higher count

Steps are distance divided by step length. On the formula in the [1 mile guide](/guides/how-many-steps-is-1-mile), a shorter step means more steps for the same street, driveways included. A child walking next to you covers your route in more footfalls than you do. Write both counts down. They measure one pavement at two step lengths.

## Cross at the corner, and be visible

[NHTSA’s Halloween tips](https://www.nhtsa.gov/halloween-safety-tips) tell pedestrians to use a buddy system, follow signs and signals, and, when you can, cross at a crosswalk or an intersection while looking for cars in all directions, including those turning left or right. At night, [NHTSA’s page on pedestrians and children](https://www.nhtsa.gov/stay-vigilant-pedestrians-and-children) says wear reflective materials or use a flashlight.

## Compare the three counts

After the last porch, line up the kid, the adult beside them, and the friend who walked the driveways while everyone else waited at the door. A hive can hold those three totals in one place. If the group wants the week-long version, that is the [10,000-step Halloween challenge](/guides/10000-step-halloween-challenge).

After Halloween, [walking after the clocks go back](/guides/walking-after-the-clocks-go-back) is the daily walk for the darker evenings that follow.
